The cap table said peer. The whiteboard said teacher.

I let those two descriptions sit beside each other for months. We were building as equals, and I was teaching things I needed the other person to be able to do. I would check the work before building on it, find what still needed explaining, and go through it. Then I would return to my own part, which had been waiting while I did that.

Teaching did not bother me in itself. What bothered me was needing the teaching to be finished so that we could do the work I thought we had agreed to share. The explanation had a deadline attached to it, even when I did not say so. I wanted to be patient, and I needed the thing to work now.

I did not know how to say that without sounding like I was announcing myself the better person.

So I kept the arrangement unnamed. I told myself I was sparing someone a cruel conversation. But leaving it unnamed did not make the difference go away. It made each piece of unfinished work carry the conversation I had refused to have.

Checking became a second job. Someone saying a task was done no longer meant I could move on to the next one. I had to look underneath it first. When I did not want to explain why I was checking again, I could quietly redo the work at midnight. That got the task further along and left me more resentful. By the next discussion I was bringing in the hours I had spent alone, including hours I had never made visible.

I cannot tell you exactly how the other person understood all of this. I know how quickly I began hearing a disagreement about the work as resistance to being taught. Once I had put myself in the teacher's place, it was easy to hear every defense as evidence that the lesson had not landed. I could be right about an error and still use that error to stop listening.

There are emails from my early twenties that I still cannot reread.

In them I was asking for trust I had not yet given anyone much reason to extend. I wanted someone to recognize what I could become and let that recognition stand in for what I could do already. I remember how urgent it felt. Waiting to be taken seriously could feel like being prevented from becoming the person I knew I was going to be.

I do not enjoy meeting that person in my sent mail. I enjoy it even less when I recognize him in someone whose work I am checking.

That recognition does not finish the task or give me back the evening. I can remember being inexperienced and still be tired of carrying work I expected to share. Someone taking a chance on me mattered enormously. So did the time they spent making that chance possible. I did not always know how much of it there was.

I think this is part of why the conversation was so difficult. I knew what it was like to hear that I was not ready. I did not want to become the person saying it. But I had turned that reluctance into an arrangement where I expected the other person to catch up without our having agreed what catching up meant, or how much of my time it would take.

And there was something else keeping the arrangement comfortable enough to continue: we could still make each other feel good about it.

I know the little high of showing someone a demo and watching their face light up. I have gone looking for it. For a while, their excitement makes the work feel more solid. I can go back to the same generous audience when I need that feeling again, without having learned much about the part that might fail.

That does not make their praise false. A friend can be pleased for me. Someone new to a field can ask the question I have stopped noticing. Neither needs an impressive title to notice something real. The trouble starts when I use the warmth of a response to answer a question the response never addressed. Being excited together did not tell us whether we could depend on each other's work.

Our enthusiasm gave us an easy conversation to keep having. The difficult one was still there when I went back to the whiteboard.

The distinction I need here is between agreeing to teach and silently absorbing the teaching into a workload I thought we had divided. People teach each other inside good partnerships all the time. I need to learn things too. A difference in experience does not by itself decide who gets to be an equal.

What I could not keep doing was treating the equality on paper as if it had already answered every question about our daily responsibilities. It had not answered who would check the work, what we could each finish independently, or what would happen when the teaching took longer than I could give it. I had answers in my head. I was getting angry that the work did not follow them.

There was a conversation available that did not require me to pass a verdict on the whole person. I could have described what I was doing, what I needed help carrying, and what I could no longer promise. It might still have been painful. We might not have found an arrangement that worked. I cannot make the missed conversation guarantee a better ending.

But I left it unsaid because I thought naming the difference would diminish someone. Then I went on working with that difference every day, privately, resentfully, letting it explain more and more of the person to me.

That is the part I keep returning to. I wanted to protect their dignity. I also wanted to avoid being the one who said the difficult thing. Those were not always the same wish.
